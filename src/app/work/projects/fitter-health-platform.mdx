## The problem

A Spain-based preventive health team had a validated product concept, real
clinical domain expertise and no engineering function. They did not need a
prototype or a contractor to work a ticket queue. They needed the platform
built, and one person accountable for all of it.

The code and the model are under NDA. What follows is architecture and
reasoning, with no implementation detail or client data.

## Constraints

The hard part here was never the stack. It was the domain.

- **Memberships are entitlements, not subscriptions.** A member holds a balance of credits that different session types consume at different rates, with expiry and carry-over rules. Booking has to check entitlement, not just availability.
- **Two-sided booking.** Coaches and members each have calendars, and a session is only real when both sides hold. Cancellation has to return the credit under some conditions and not others.
- **Invoice state lives in Odoo.** Payment status originates in more than one payment rail and has to reconcile against the platform's view of what a member is owed or owes.
- **Clinical compliance.** Who may see which record is a rule with legal weight, which produces a role and permission matrix considerably larger than the usual admin, staff, user triple.
- **One engineer.** Every hour spent hand-rolling infrastructure was an hour not spent on the domain above.

## Architecture

Django and Django REST Framework behind a Next.js frontend, PostgreSQL as the
primary store, Celery and Redis for anything that must not block a request. On
AWS: ECS behind API Gateway, RDS, S3, SNS and SQS for eventing, Redshift on the
analytics path, inside a VPC with scoped IAM roles.

## Decisions

**Django over FastAPI.** With one engineer and a non-technical team, the admin,
the migration system and the auth stack removed months of work I would otherwise
have written myself. It also lets the founders inspect and correct production
records without filing a ticket with me, which for an entitlement model with
edge cases is worth more than throughput. FastAPI would have been faster per
request and considerably slower to a working business.

**Entitlement state in the database, not in application logic.** Credit balances
are money-adjacent. Every rule that could be expressed as a constraint or a
transaction is, because application-layer validation is one deploy away from
being bypassed and a double-spent credit is a support conversation with a member
who is already annoyed.

**ECS over Lambda.** Predictable long-lived database connections, no cold start
on clinician-facing endpoints, and a monthly cost the founders could forecast
before committing to it.

## What went wrong

The inactivity re-engagement pipeline dropped notifications, silently. Members
who should have been nudged after a period without a session sometimes were not,
and nothing in the logs said so. It surfaced as a product complaint, not as an
alert, which is the worst way for anything to surface.

The cause was the shape of the fan-out. SNS was publishing straight to its
consumers, and SNS delivery is fire and forget: if a consumer was down,
throttled or slow when the message arrived, the retry policy eventually gave up
and the message was gone. There was no buffer holding it, so there was nothing
to inspect afterwards and no count that disagreed with any other count. My first
instinct was that the scheduling job was not selecting the right members, and I
spent real time verifying the query before accepting that the messages were
being generated correctly and lost after publication.

The fix was to put SQS between SNS and the consumers. The topic now fans out to
queues, and consumers read from the queue. A message survives a consumer being
unavailable, redelivery is the default rather than a best effort, and anything
that repeatedly fails lands somewhere I can look at it instead of disappearing.
The lesson generalised: I now treat a queue without a durable buffer and a
dead-letter path as an outage that has not happened yet.

## Results

- Live in production at [fitter.health](https://fitter.health/), built and operated by one engineer
- Entitlement, booking and invoice reconciliation modelled with database-level constraints, so a credit cannot be double-spent by a race between two requests
- Notification delivery is durable and inspectable after the SNS to SQS change, with failures landing in a dead-letter queue rather than vanishing
- Infrastructure defined and reproducible, and the client owns the AWS account, the repositories and the domains

## What I would do differently

I would have built the notification path with the queue in it from the first
day. The SNS-direct version was not a shortcut I took knowingly, it was a
default I did not question, and questioning it would have cost an hour against
the days it eventually took to find. Anything asynchronous and user-visible now
starts with the buffer and the dead-letter path, and earns the right to be
simpler later.
